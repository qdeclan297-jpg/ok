# Part 6: dividends, and the Philippines goal

Two questions that turn out to be the same question: you asked about dividend
funds, and you want to live in the Philippines. Income funds *feel* like the
route to living off your money. The data says otherwise, and the gap costs you
years.

Numbers from `research/09_dividend_funds.py` and `research/10_philippines_goal.py`.

---

## 1. Accumulating vs distributing is not a return decision

VWRP and VWRL are the *same fund* on the same index. One reinvests dividends
inside the fund, the other pays them to you as cash.

| | Return | Vol | Max DD |
|---|---|---|---|
| VWRP (accumulating) | 11.71%/yr | 14.8% | 25.1% |
| VWRL (distributing) | 11.70%/yr | 14.8% | 25.0% |

**Identical.** A dividend isn't free money — the fund's price drops by exactly
the dividend on the ex-dividend date. You're moving money from one pocket to
another, not creating it.

While you're accumulating, **accumulating is simply less work**: no cash to
sweep up, no re-buying, no FX fees on small amounts. Inside an ISA the tax
treatment is identical too.

## 2. Dividend-*focused* funds have underperformed

This is different from acc/dist. These funds deliberately tilt toward high-yield
stocks, and that tilt has cost real money.

**US funds, common window since 2011:**

| Fund | Total return | Vol | Max DD |
|---|---|---|---|
| S&P 500 (VOO) | **15.00%/yr** | 16.7% | 34.0% |
| Total US (VTI) | **14.65%/yr** | 17.0% | 35.0% |
| Schwab US Dividend (SCHD) | 13.33%/yr | 15.4% | 33.4% |
| Dividend Appreciation (VIG) | 12.77%/yr | 15.0% | 31.7% |
| High Dividend Yield (VYM) | 12.55%/yr | 15.1% | 35.2% |
| Dividend Aristocrats (NOBL) | 10.41%/yr | 15.8% | 35.4% |

**GBP funds, common window since 2013:**

| Fund | Total return | Vol | Max DD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanguard All-World (VWRL) | **11.23%/yr** | 14.2% | 25.0% |
| Vanguard All-World High Div Yield (VHYL) | 9.23%/yr | 13.1% | 27.9% |
| Vanguard FTSE 100 (VUKE) | 7.21%/yr | 14.9% | 34.3% |
| iShares UK Dividend (IUKD) | **0.99%/yr** | 16.9% | 54.9% |

Every dividend fund lost to its broad-market equivalent. **The All-World gap is
2.00 percentage points per year.** Compounded on £10,000 over 34 years that's
£372,743 plain against £201,390 high-dividend — **£171,353 of difference.**

**The fair point in their favour:** dividend funds were slightly less volatile
(13.1% vs 14.2% for VHYL). You gave up 2 points of return for about 1 point of
volatility. That's a poor trade while you're accumulating.

Note IUKD's 0.99%/yr. Chasing the highest yields selects for companies in
trouble — a high yield often means the price collapsed, not that the business
is generous.

## 4. When £300/month gets you there — and why it isn't enough

At £300/month indexed to inflation, in today's money:

| Target | @5% real | @6.6% real |
|---|---|---|
| £271k (lean, 3.5%) | age 58 | age 54 |
| £355k (Cebu, 4%) | **age 62** | age 57 |
| £406k (Cebu, 3.5%) | age 64 | age 59 |

**That's a normal retirement, not an early move.** £300/month is a good start at
26, but on its own it does not get you to the Philippines early.

The contribution is the lever that matters most:

| Monthly | £271k (lean) | £355k (Cebu 4%) | £406k (Cebu 3.5%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| £300 | age 58 | age 62 | age 64 |
| £500 | age 50 | age 54 | age 56 |
| **£750** | **age 45** | **age 48** | **age 50** |
| £1,000 | age 41 | age 44 | age 46 |
| £1,500 | age 37 | age 40 | age 41 |

You're 26. Earnings usually rise substantially between 26 and 40. **Every
increase you can make early buys years at the other end** — going from £300 to
£750 moves the Cebu date from 62 to 48.

### And this is why the dividend question matters

| £300/mo to £355,000 | Reach it at |
|---|---|
| At 5% real (broad global fund) | age 62 |
| At 3% real (2-point dividend drag) | **age 72** |

**A 2-point drag doesn't cost you money, it costs you a decade in the
Philippines.** At £750/month it's still 4 years.

## 5. Four things about this goal that change the earlier advice

### ⚠️ The LISA is mostly wrong for you — correcting what I said before

I recommended a LISA on the basis of the first-home bonus. For your goal that
was wrong on two counts:

1. **The LISA first-home benefit applies to UK property only.** It cannot buy a
   home in the Philippines.
2. **Foreigners cannot own land in the Philippines at all.** The constitution
   restricts land to Filipino citizens and 60%-Filipino companies. You can own a
   condominium unit outright, but foreign ownership of any building is capped at
   40%, so your options are limited to buildings under that ceiling.

So the LISA is only useful to you as a **locked-until-60 pot**. That still earns
the 25% bonus and is still a genuinely good deal — but it is the opposite of
what you need if you want to leave at 45–50. Money in a LISA cannot fund ages
45–60 without a 25% penalty that wipes out the bonus and more.

**If leaving early is the goal, prioritise the accessible Stocks & Shares ISA.**

### The pension has the same problem, only more so

A pension is untouchable until 57–58. If your target is to be in the Philippines
at 48, a pension cannot fund years 48–57. You'd need a **bridge**: accessible ISA
money covering the gap, with the pension taking over later.

The employer match is still free money and still worth taking. But don't put
*everything* there if you want to leave before 57.

### Philippine tax is unusually kind here

**Aliens — resident or not — are taxed only on Philippine-source income.** Your
UK/Irish fund dividends and capital gains are foreign-source, so the Philippines
generally won't tax them at all.

This matters because the Philippines doesn't recognise the ISA wrapper — but it
doesn't need to. Most European countries would tax your ISA as normal income.
The Philippines shouldn't tax it at all. **Your destination is one of the more
favourable ones for this.**

### You can't pay into an ISA once you leave

Once you stop being UK tax resident, **new ISA contributions stop**. Existing
holdings stay sheltered and keep growing, but you can't add.

That makes the next ~15–20 years, while you're UK resident, your **only window**
to fill the ISA. Use it hard.

### The visa itself is not the obstacle

The SRRV retiree visa minimum age dropped to **40**, with deposits from **USD
1,500**, and the deposit is refundable. It's cheap and it's not the binding
constraint — your pot is. Before 40 you'd need a different visa route (work,
marriage, or rolling tourist extensions).

## 6. So what should you actually hold?

**While accumulating (now until you leave):** one broad global accumulating fund,
in a Stocks & Shares ISA, on a zero-fee platform. Not a dividend fund. You don't
need income yet, and the tilt costs 2 points a year — which is years of your life.

**When you get there:** you have two ways to draw an income, and they're
equivalent in the Philippines because neither is taxed:

- **Sell units as needed** (total-return approach). More efficient, gives you
  full control over how much you draw, and doesn't force a 2-point drag on your
  whole portfolio for decades beforehand.
- **Switch to distributing funds** near the date if you prefer cash landing
  automatically. Converting later costs you nothing in an ISA and avoids the
  drag during accumulation.

Either way, **the dividend decision belongs at age 45, not age 26.**

---

## 7. The honest summary

- £300/month gets you to the Philippines around **60**. That's retirement, not
  escape.
- £750/month gets you there around **48**. The contribution is the lever, not
  the fund choice.
- Dividend funds would delay you by **4–10 years**. Avoid them while accumulating.
- The **LISA is not the right tool** for your goal, despite what I said earlier —
  it locks money until 60 and its housing benefit is UK-only.
- Prioritise the **accessible ISA**, because early departure needs accessible
  money and because you lose the ability to contribute the day you leave.
- The Philippines' territorial tax system is genuinely favourable to this plan.

---

## 8. What I still can't tell you

- **Your income and whether £300 can become £750.** This is now the single
  biggest variable in the entire plan.
- **Whether your employer matches pension contributions**, and how that trades
  off against needing accessible money before 57.
- **Currency risk.** You'd hold GBP/USD assets and spend pesos. A 20% adverse
  move in GBP/PHP is a 20% pay cut, and none of the figures here model it.
- **Healthcare.** Not in these budgets, and it becomes a larger cost as you age.
- **Whether you'd actually like living there long term.** Spend extended time
  there before committing a plan this long to it.

Not financial advice — and for the tax and visa side specifically, this is worth
confirming with someone qualified in both jurisdictions before you act on it.
